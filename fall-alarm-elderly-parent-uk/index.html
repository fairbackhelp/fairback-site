Choosing a fall alarm for an elderly parent (UK): what each type misses

Families often buy an alarm after a fall, then find it sits in a drawer or doesn't go off when it matters. No alarm detects every fall. The aim is to pick the one your parent will actually wear, and that fits how they live.

Start with the free and local options

  • Council community alarms. Many councils run a pendant service linked to a 24-hour response centre, sometimes with someone who can come out. Apply through GOV.UK "Apply for a community alarm" or your council. Some charge a small weekly fee.
  • A free care needs assessment from your council's adult social care team (England). If your parent is assessed as needing them, aids and minor adaptations costing up to £1,000 each are provided free.
  • The landline switch-off. Older alarms that plug into a landline may stop working as phone lines move to digital. If your parent has one, tell their phone provider and the alarm company (Citizens Advice has a guide).

The main types, and what each misses

1. Pendant or wristband button with a base unit

  • Good for: someone who will press a button and is mostly at home.
  • Misses: falls where the person is confused, unconscious or doesn't think to press it. Families describe parents lying on the floor for hours wearing an unpressed pendant.
  • Check: range from the base unit (thick walls and big houses can be a problem) and whether it needs a landline.

2. Automatic fall-detect pendant

  • Good for: someone who might not press a button.
  • Misses: slow falls and slumps, such as sliding off a chair or bed. Can give false alarms with sudden movements.
  • Check: can the sensitivity be adjusted? Is there a monthly monitoring fee or does it call family?

3. Fall-detect watch with built-in SIM (no base unit)

  • Good for: someone who goes out, and who would rather wear a watch than a pendant.
  • Misses: falls while it's off charging, often every 2 to 5 days. Many aren't suitable for the shower, which is where a lot of falls happen.
  • Check: battery life, water resistance, whether the strap is easy for stiff fingers, whether the alarm is loud enough without hearing aids, and GPS if they walk alone.

4. Smartwatch fall detection (e.g. Apple Watch, some Android watches)

  • Good for: someone already comfortable with a smartwatch and phone.
  • Misses: daily charging, and some need a particular phone to set up. Often too fiddly for someone new to tech.

5. Home activity sensors (no wearable)

  • Good for: someone who refuses to wear anything. Sensors learn normal routines and alert family if, say, the kitchen isn't used by mid-morning.
  • Misses: they don't detect the fall itself, so help may be slower. Need Wi-Fi.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • Will they wear it day and night, including in the shower?
  • Who gets the alert: a 24-hour response centre, or family? What happens if family can't answer?
  • Monthly fee, contract length, and how to cancel (ask about refunds if the person dies or moves into care).
  • Does it need a landline, mobile signal or Wi-Fi at home?
  • Can a key safe be fitted so responders can get in?

Where to look

Compare features yourself and check each seller's terms: fall-detect pendants, fall-detect watches with a SIM, police-approved key safes and motion-sensor night lights (for night-time trips to the bathroom). Ask your parent's GP or an occupational therapist if falls are frequent: repeated falls can have a medical cause worth checking.
